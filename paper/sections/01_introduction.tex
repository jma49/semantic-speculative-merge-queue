\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Consider two maintainers changing an interface. One widens the range returned by a parser. The other adds a caller that assumes the old range. Separate CI jobs can both pass against the same revision. A text merge can also succeed, even though running the caller with the new parser violates its assumption. A queue that batches the two changes can discover the interaction early, at the expense of delaying both. A queue that separates them can produce two green results while the interaction is still untested. Which changes to group and what a passing result authorizes are distinct engineering questions.

Batch testing amortizes CI startup and execution costs across changes~\cite{fallahzadeh2023accelerating}. At a busy integration branch, speculative execution also hides latency by preparing candidates while earlier work is outstanding~\cite{ananthanarayanan2019keeping}. Both techniques transfer some cost to recovery. Bisection spends tests to localize a failed batch. A transient failure can make a correct singleton appear defective. A successful test can become stale while another worker advances the branch. The relative importance of these costs depends on the workload. The queue operator observes changed files and CI output much more readily than the actual dependency between future failures.

We study the Semantic Speculative Merge Queue (SSMQ), a policy that combines a head-anchored batch packer with bounded retries and a single traceback-guided split. Its structural predictor is deliberately simple: it estimates files affected by each change from historical co-change and directory membership. A queue may use that estimate to spend test capacity differently. Authority to commit comes from verification of the resulting state.

We ask three questions. First, how often do public merge sequences admit a batch with disjoint directory footprints? Second, when do the response-time and resource costs of the proposed policy improve on FIFO, conflict-aware batching, and dependency-aware speculation under identical offered workloads? Third, on an executable queue, what does a traceback-guided split add at fixed packing, and when does accepting a stale result break the target branch? The first question uses observational data, the second varies assumptions in a simulator, and the third uses a small program with known faults. The separation matters: none of the public traces records a queue's rejected changes, its workers' test outcomes, or the failures a different ordering would have produced.
