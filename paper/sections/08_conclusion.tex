\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A speculative merge queue makes decisions with two kinds of information: inexpensive structural estimates before execution and stronger, revision-specific evidence after execution. The public merge sequences establish that opportunities to change a batch depend on the repository. Controlled scheduling experiments quantify the consequences under stated assumptions about faults, and executable Git experiments distinguish packing from failure recovery. A change to the target branch can invalidate an otherwise correct test result. The practical question left open is whether the cost of structural false positives, rechecks, and rejected changes is justified by their benefit on a deployment's own queue.
